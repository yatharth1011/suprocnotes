## **Carbonyl Compounds**

### **1.0 Classification And General Introduction**

Organic Compounds having $>C=O$ group are called carbonyl compounds and $>C=O$ group is known as carbonyl or oxo group. It's general formula is $C_n H_{2n} O$ (n = 1, 2, 3......). Carbonyl compounds are grouped into two categories.

**(a) Aldehydes:** Aldehyde group is $-\stackrel{\huge\text{O}}{||}{\text{C}}-H$ (also known as formyl group). It is a monovalent group.

**(b) Ketones:** The carbonyl group ($>C=O$) is a Ketonic group when its both the valencies are satisfied by alkyl group. It is a bivalent group.

**Structure**
In $>C=O$ compounds C-atom is $sp^2$ hybridised which forms two $\sigma$ bonds with C and H-atom respectively and one $\sigma$ bond with oxygen atom. The unhybridised atomic orbital of C-atom and the parallel 2p orbital of oxygen atom give the $\pi$ bond in $>C=O$ group.

Due to electro-negativity difference in C & O atoms, the $>C=O$ group is polar.
$>\overset{\delta+}{C} = \overset{\delta-}{O}$ Hence aldehydes and Ketones posses dipole moment.

### **2.0 GENERAL METHODS OF PREPARATION**

### **2.1 For BOTH ALDEHYDES AND KETONES**

#### **(a) By Oxidation (or dehydrogenation) of Alcohols**
(a) By $K_2Cr_2O_7 / H_2SO_4$
Oxidation of primary alcohols gives aldehyde and oxidation of secondary alcohols gives Ketones. Here, ($K_2Cr_2O_7 / H_2SO_4$) is a strong oxidising agent.

$RCH_2OH \xrightarrow{K_2Cr_2O_7 / H_2SO_4 (dil)} RCHO$ (Aldehyde)

$R-\underset{OH}{\underset{|}{CH}}-R \xrightarrow{K_2Cr_2O_7 / H_2SO_4} R-\stackrel{\huge\text{O}}{||}{\text{C}}-R$ (Ketone)

Aldehydes are quite susceptible to further oxidation to acids -
$RCH_2OH \xrightarrow{[O]} R-CHO \xrightarrow{[O]} R-COOH$
Thus oxidation of primary alcohols is made at the temperature much above the boiling point of aldehyde and thus aldehydes are vapourised out and prevented from being oxidised.
**Note:** Aldehydes can be prepared from 1°-alcohols, secondary alcohols can be oxidized to ketones, by oxidation with pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC) in $CH_2Cl_2$ solvent, pyridinium dichromate (PDC) and with Collin's reagent ($CrO_3$ . pyridin + sol)

#### **(b) Oppenauer Oxidation**
The oxidation of secondary alcohols to ketones by heating them with specific reagent: ($[(CH_3)_3CO]_3Al$) (Aluminium t-butoxide) in presence of acetone. Primary alcohols may also be oxidized to aldehydes, if acetone is replaced by a better hydrogen acceptor, (e.g. p-benzoquinone), the equilibrium can be controlled by the amount of acetone, an excess of which favours the oxidation of the alcohol.

$R_2CHOH + CH_3-\stackrel{\huge\text{O}}{||}{\text{C}}-CH_3 \rightleftharpoons [(CH_3)_3CO]_3Al \rightleftharpoons R_2C=O + CH_3-\underset{OH}{\underset{|}{CH}}-CH_3$
2° Alcohol $\quad$ Acetone $\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad$ Ketone $\quad$ Isopropyl alcohol

$RCH_2OH + \text{[p-Benzoquinone]} \xrightarrow{[(CH_3)_3CO]_3Al} R-CHO + \text{[Quinol]}$
1° Alcohol $\quad$ Quinone $\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad$ Aldehyde $\quad$ Quinol

### **3.0 FOR ALDEHYDES ONLY**

### **3.1 Reduction of acyl chlorides, esters and nitriles**
Acyl chlorides, esters and nitriles can be reduced to aldehydes by treating them with lithium-tri-tert-butoxyaluminium hydride ($LiAlH[OC(CH_3)_3]_3$) and DIBAL-H(di iso propyl aluminium hydride) at – 78°C.

$R-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{C}}-Cl \xrightarrow{\text{(i)} LiAlH[OC(CH_3)_3]_3} \xrightarrow{\text{(ii)} H_2O} R-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{C}}-H$

Both esters and nitriles can be reduced to aldehydes by DIBAL-H. Reduction must be carried out at low temperatures. Hydrolysis of the intermediates gives the aldehyde.

(i) $R-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{C}}-OR' \xrightarrow{\text{(i) DIBAL-H, Hexane, -78°C}} \xrightarrow{\text{(ii)} H_2O} R-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{C}}-H$

(ii) $R-C \equiv N \xrightarrow{\text{(i) DIBAL-H, Hexane, -78°C}} \xrightarrow{\text{(ii)} H_2O} R-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{C}}-H$

### **3.2 Rosenmund's reduction**
Quinoline or sulphur act as a poisoned catalyst, controls the further reduction of aldehyde to alcohols.

$RCOCl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd / BaSO_4, \text{Quinoline or sulphur}} RCHO + HCl$

$RCOCl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd} RCHO \rightarrow RCH_2OH$
Formaldehyde can not be prepared by this method.

**Example:** $C_2H_5COCl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd / BaSO_4, \text{Quinoline or sulphur}} C_2H_5CHO + HCl$
$\quad$ Propionyl Chloride $\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad$ Propanal

### **3.3 Stephen's reduction**

$R-C \equiv N \xrightarrow{SnCl_2 / HCl} R-CH=NH \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} RCHO + NH_3$

$C_2H_5-C \equiv N \xrightarrow{SnCl_2 / HCl} C_2H_5CH=NH \xrightarrow{H_3O^+} C_2H_5CHO + NH_3$

### **6.0 Physical Properties of Carbonyl Compounds :**
*   **Solubility**
    Aldehyde and ketones having upto 3 carbons (formaldehyde, acetaldehyde and propionaldehyde and acetone) are quite soluble in water due to polarity of $>C=O$ bond and can form H—bond with water molecule. Aldehydes and ketones having equal to or more than five carbons are insoluble in water.
    
    $>\overset{\delta-}{C=O} \cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot H^{\delta+}-O^{\delta-}_{\Large H^{\delta+}}$
    
    H-bonding
    
    Solubility $\propto \frac{1}{\text{Molecular weight}}$
    
*   **Boiling point**
    Boiling point $\propto$ Molecular weight
    
    Boiling point order is - **Alcohol > Carbonyl compounds > Alkane**
    
    This is because in alcohols intermolecular H-bonding is present but in carbonyl compounds H-bonding doesn't exist, instead dipole-dipole attraction and vander waal force of attraction is present. Alkanes are non polar.
    
    $>\overset{\delta+}{C}=\overset{\delta-}{O} \cdot\cdot\cdot\cdot \overset{\delta+}{C}=\overset{\delta-}{O}>$

### **7.0 CHEMICAL PROPERTIES**

### **7.1 Nucleophilic Addition Reactions of Carbonyl Compounds**
Due to strong electronegativity of oxygen, the mobile $\pi$ electrons pulled strongly towards oxygen, leaving the carbon atom deficient of electrons.

$>\text{C=O} \leftrightarrow >\overset{\oplus}{\text{C}}-\overset{\ominus}{\text{O}}$

Carbon is thus readily attacked by $\overset{\ominus}{Nu}$. The negatively charged oxygen is attacked by electron deficient (electrophile) $E^+$.

$>C=O$ bond in carbonyl group is stronger than C=C bond in alkanes.

| Bond       | Bond Energy   | Bond       | Bond Energy   |
|------------|---------------|------------|---------------|
| C—O        | 84.0 K Cals   | C—C        | 83.1 K Cals   |
| C=O        | 178 K Cals    | C=C        | 146 K Cals    |

**(Reactivity of carbonyl group $\propto$ Magnitude of +ve charge)**

### **7.2 Nucleophilic Addition Reactions**
*   **Addition of HCN**
    $>\text{C=O} + \text{HCN} \rightarrow >\underset{CN}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{OH}$ (Cyanohydrin)
    
    The cyanohydrin product can further react:
    - $\xrightarrow{H_2O, \text{Partial hydrolysis}} >\underset{CONH_2}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{OH}$ ($\alpha$-Hydroxy amide)
    - $\xrightarrow{H_3O^\oplus, \text{Complete hydrolysis}} >\underset{COOH}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{OH}$ ($\alpha$-Hydroxy acid)
    - $\xrightarrow{Ni, H_2} >\underset{CH_2NH_2}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{OH}$ ($\beta$-Amino alcohol)
    
*   **Addition of NaHSO$_3$**
    This reaction is utilized for the separation of carbonyl compounds from non - carbonyl compounds.
    
    $>\text{C=O} + \text{NaHSO}_3 \rightarrow >\underset{SO_3Na}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{OH} \xrightarrow{H_3O^\oplus} \text{Carbonyl compounds. *(Regenerated)}}$
    Sodium bi sulphite $\quad$ Bisulphite compound (Crystalline)
    
    **Mechanism:**
    $\text{NaHSO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{Na}^+ + \text{HSO}_3^-$
    $\text{HSO}_3^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{SO}_3^{2-}$
    
    $>\text{C=O} + \text{SO}_3^{2-} \xrightarrow{\text{slow}} >\underset{SO_3^\ominus}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{O}^\ominus \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+, \text{fast}} >\underset{SO_3^\ominus}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{Na}^+} >\underset{SO_3Na}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-\text{OH}$
    
    Only Aldehyde and Methyl Ketones can give this reaction.

### **1.6 KNOEVENAGEL CONDENSATION**
Reaction of active methylene group with aldehyde & Ketones is known as knoevenagel reaction.
$RCHO + CH_2(Z)_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Pyridine or piperidine}} RCH=C(COOR)_2 \xrightarrow{\text{(i) }H_2O/H^\oplus} \xrightarrow{\text{(ii) }\Delta, -CO_2} R-CH=CH-COOH$

**Z can be**
CHO, COOMe, CN, NO$_2$, COR, SOR, SO$_2$R, SO$_2$OR etc.

**Mechanism :**
The base (e.g., piperidine) removes a proton from the active methylene compound to form a carbanion. This carbanion then acts as a nucleophile, attacking the carbonyl carbon of the aldehyde. The resulting adduct eliminates water to form the $\alpha, \beta$-unsaturated product.

$R-CH=C(COOR)_2 \xrightarrow{\text{(i) }H_2O/H^\oplus} \xrightarrow{\text{(ii) }\Delta, -CO_2} R-CH=CHCOOH$
High reactivity of the methylene group of the active methylene compound prevents self-condensation of the aldehyde.

$\alpha$-halo esters when treated with Zn it gives organometallic halo ester which provides the attacking Nu for the another reactant, which is a carbonyl compound. When Nu$^-$ attacks on carbonyl compound it gives an intermediate which upon acidic hydrolysis followed by heating, results in formation of $\alpha, \beta$- unsaturated acid. The overall reaction is known as Reformatsky reaction.

$Ph-CHO + CH_2(Br)-COOEt \xrightarrow{Zn, H_2O/\Delta} Ph-CH=CH-COOH$
Benzaldehyde $\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad$ Cinnamic acid

**Mechanism :**
The zinc reacts with the $\alpha$-bromo ester to form an organozinc compound (a zinc enolate). This enolate then adds to the carbonyl group of the aldehyde. Acidic workup protonates the alkoxide, and subsequent heating causes dehydration to give the final $\alpha, \beta$-unsaturated acid.
$Ph-CH(OH)-CH_2-COOH \xrightarrow{\Delta, -H_2O} Ph-CH=CH-COOH$
Cinnamic acid

### **1.8 BENZIL-BENZILIC REARRANGEMENT OR BENZILIC ACID REARRANGEMENT**
The base catalysed reaction of 1,2-diketone to a salt of $\alpha$-hydroxy carboxylic acid is known as Benzilic acid rearrangement, this reaction is mainly applicable when aryl group is present on both carbonyl carbons.

$Ph-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{\text{C}}}-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{\text{C}}}-Ph \xrightarrow{\text{(i) }OH^-} \xrightarrow{\text{(ii) }H^+} Ph_2\underset{OH}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}-COOH$
(Benzil) $\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad$ (Benzilic acid)

**Mechanism :**
Hydroxide ion attacks one carbonyl carbon. This is followed by a rearrangement where a phenyl group migrates from the adjacent carbon to the carbon attacked by OH$^-$. This generates a carboxylate and an alkoxide, which then undergoes proton transfer to form benzilate anion. Acidification gives benzilic acid.

### **1.9 MICHAEL REACTION**
**Michael Addition:** $\alpha-\beta$ unsaturated carbonyl compound undergo michael reaction with compounds having active methylene. Many different nucleophile can add to a $\alpha-\beta$ unsaturated carbonyl compound. When the nucleophile is an enolate the addition reaction has a special name MICHAEL REACTION.
**Mechanism:** The base removes a proton from the active methylene compound, forming a carbanion (enolate). This carbanion then attacks the $\beta$-carbon of the $\alpha, \beta$-unsaturated carbonyl compound in a conjugate addition.

### **1.11 BENZOIN CONDENSATION**
The benzoin condensation is essentially a dimerisation of two aromatic aldehydes under the catalytic influence of cyanide ions to give benzoin (I).
$2 \ C_6H_5CHO \xrightarrow{KCN, H_2O_2} H_5C_6-\underset{OH}{\underset{|}{\text{C}}}H-\overset{\large\text{O}}{\overset{||}{\text{C}}}-C_6H_5$
$\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad$ (I)
The hydrogen atom attached to the carbonyl group of aldehyde is not active enough to be removed easily but the addition of the cyanide ion to the carbonyl carbon places this hydrogen in the alpha position of the nitrile thus rendering it relatively acidic. The carbanion, thus generated, attacks the carbonyl carbon of the second aldehyde molecule in a rate-determining step forming an unstable cyanohydrin of benzoin which immediately breaks down into benzoin and hydrogen cyanide.

**Solution**
The mechanism involves nucleophilic attack of CN$^-$ on the carbonyl carbon. Electron-withdrawing groups (like -NO$_2$) increase the positive charge on the carbonyl carbon, making it more electrophilic and thus increasing the rate of reaction. Electron-donating groups decrease the rate.
Benzoin condensation is due to stability of intermediate (III) when negative charge on C extensively delocalised in benzene ring, nitro and C$\equiv$N group. In all other cases, such dispersal is not extensively possible. On the other hand, -NO$_2$ - is also creating a positive charge center on carbonyl carbon, making it more susceptible to nucleophilic attack of CN$^-$.
